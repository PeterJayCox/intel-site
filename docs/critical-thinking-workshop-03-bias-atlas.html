A bias is a mechanism, and it leaves a mark in the text. That is what this session trains: seeing the mark, not reciting the name. Twenty-one mechanisms are listed below. Each has a tell you can point at and a move that takes minutes. Once you know the tells, a draft becomes readable the way a source does under Admiralty Rating. You stop asking how it feels and start asking what it rests on.

Bottom line up front🔗

The wiki names twenty-one cognitive biases, and this session walks all of them. Five structural patterns sit beside them, and those are the rows nobody can fix alone. This is not a vocabulary test. Knowing that a draft shows confirmation bias changes nothing. Knowing which sentence produced it changes the draft, and so does knowing the one move that answers it.

Two warnings, because this material gets misused in two ways. The first is treating bias as dishonesty. Every official inquiry into the Iraq WMD failure found no deliberate distortion, and the judgement was still wrong. The mechanisms that produced it are the ordinary ones we all run.2 The second is using bias as a synonym for a view you disagree with. If everything is bias, nothing is, and a checklist that fires on every draft is one nobody runs.

Format: 90 minutes, facilitated, one team, discussion throughout. Material: a short constructed draft to hunt through, then your own work. Output: a signature card you keep, and a map from bias to technique to artefact that you can put in front of a manager.

Why this session exists🔗

Workshop 02 — Iraq's WMD: A Bias Post-Mortem works these mechanisms hard inside one case, with the inquiries’ own words. That is the right shape for a case study. It is not a reference. Workshop 02 asks what the mechanisms did to one assessment; it does not give you the list. At four on a Friday, with your own draft open, you are not going to re-derive the Iraq judgements. You need the mechanisms in front of you, grouped so you can find the one that is operating.

This session does two things Workshop 02 does not. It covers the full taxonomy, including the biases that never fired in Iraq and that nobody in this room has a case study for. And it separates cognitive bias, which an analyst can catch in their own draft, from structural bias, which no amount of care fixes because it sits in the team and the collection rather than in the person.

Each atlas row has three layers, and the session walks them in order: what the mechanism is, how it damages an assessment, and what it costs you. The third is the one people remember.

Open with the clip🔗

Source link, if you prefer to watch off-platform: youtube.com/watch?v=LRFMuGBP15U

Play the first 54 seconds before you say anything about bias. A country-house murder, played twice, with 21 changes between the two passes. Stop at the card that asks whether you noticed them. The rest of the clip is a making-of and a road-safety end card, and neither belongs in this session.

Then ask what changed. Most people name two or three. Some name none. Ask who was watching carefully, and let the answer sit, because everyone was. The task was to follow a murder, and following it is what stopped them seeing the room.

Draw out two things, and head off one.

  • Attention is a filter, not a spotlight. You attend to what the task asks for, and the rest does not enter. That is the mechanism behind Selective Perception, and it is why that row's tell is an absence: the change was in the frame and never made it into the account.
  • "No change" is a claim about the observer. The scene changed 21 times while everyone in the room was watching it. Hold that against Carried-Forward Assessment: a standing judgement that nothing has moved is a statement about what we looked at, not about the world.
  • Do not let it become a trick. The room was not unobservant. It was doing exactly what it was asked to do. Say that before anyone gets defensive, or the exercise teaches the wrong lesson.

Then the two warnings, and on with the session.

Part 1 — what bias is, and what it is not🔗

A cognitive bias is a systematic error in judgement, not a random one. It comes from the shortcuts the brain uses to make quick sense of noisy information. Systematic is the part that matters. Random error averages out across a team and across a week. Bias points the same way every time, which is why one wrong tendency can move an assessment that a hundred more reports cannot.

Three things follow.

  • Bias survives more information. Extra reporting is filtered through the same model that produced the error, so the picture gets more confident as it gets more selective. Collecting more is not the correction; changing the method is. Heuer’s formulation, quoted on Cognitive Bias: the analyst constructs meaning rather than receiving it.1
  • Bias is invisible while it operates. Nobody experiences themselves discounting disconfirming reporting. The judgement feels like a straight reading of the file. That is why the countermeasures are written checks, recorded judgements and named roles, rather than resolutions to try harder. The clip at the start of the session is the same thing at full size: nobody in the room felt themselves not seeing the changes.
  • Bias is a working function with a cost. Matching against experience is what makes an experienced analyst fast and usually right. Left unexamined, the same machinery produces the failures in the atlas. What we manage is the systematic character of the error, not the fact of it.

The BLUF warning, spelled out, because it is how this session itself fails:

Before you call a bias, say what in the draft produced it and what the other reading would have been. If you cannot do both, you have not found a bias. You have found a conclusion you dislike.

Part 2 — the two families, and how to read the atlas🔗

The taxonomy in Cognitive Bias has five families, all of them cognitive: mechanisms inside a head. The Iraq case shows something those five families hide, so this session splits the material a second way.

  • Cognitive bias, in the analyst. Confirmation, anchoring, mirror imaging, groupthink. An individual can catch these in their own draft, or a team process can catch them in someone else’s.
  • Structural bias, in the system. Single-source dependency, a two-analyst team on a national question, inherited partner reporting, "no change" carried forward without review, volume mistaken for development. These are properties of the posture. An analyst working inside them cannot fix them by being more careful. They need a decision from somebody with authority over the team or the collection.

The distinction changes what you do about it. A cognitive bias in your own draft is a ten-minute job you own. A structural one is the start of a conversation with somebody who can act on it. That is why the rows carry a family tag, and why Exercise 3 asks which ones need another person’s agreement.

Read the atlas as a signature list. Each row gives the mechanism, the damage it does to an assessment, and the shortest recognisable instance, so you can match the next one. In your own draft a description will not help you. A phrase will.

The atlas🔗

Twenty-one cognitive biases in the five families. The five structural patterns are tabulated separately because they are a different kind of object. A cognitive row runs mechanism, damage, instance. A structural row runs pattern, damage, owner.

Perception and mindset🔗

Bias What it is How it bites an assessment The example
Confirmation Bias Seeking, interpreting and recalling information in ways that support an existing view, while discounting what contradicts it. A leading hypothesis becomes self-reinforcing — each new item raises confidence without any test against a rival. The product reads as cumulative evidence when it is one idea being repeated. Every fresh report is read as consistent with the working hypothesis; the two items that cut against it are dismissed as "noise from a low-grade source".
Belief Perseverance Difficulty abandoning a mindset once formed, even after the evidence that formed it is discredited. The judgement outlives its own foundation: the source is retracted, the conclusion stays, because it has been absorbed into the picture. Worst at re-issue, where earlier judgements are carried forward without their uncertainties. A key finding still standing a year after the sourcing behind it was withdrawn, now apparently supported by everything else in the file.
Mirror Imaging Assuming the other actor shares our rationality, values and constraints. Intent is assessed from what we would do in their position. It under-estimates doctrine, culture and ideology, which is a common source of strategic surprise. "A state with nothing to hide would prove it, so the concealment must mean concealment of something" — the inference imports our own logic about disclosure.
Selective Perception Noticing what fits prior expectation and never registering the rest. The perceptual stage of confirmation bias. Contradicting reporting is present in the file and absent from the product, and nobody decided to exclude it. It operates at collection and triage too: the filter is inherited from the hypothesis rather than stated. A monitoring feed whose detection thresholds have been tuned until the alerts match the working hypothesis.

Evidence evaluation🔗

Bias What it is How it bites an assessment The example
Vividness Bias Over-weighting concrete, graphic or emotionally striking information against more diagnostic aggregate data. The memorable item carries the product: one dramatic case, one quotable figure. Sober reporting that would survive scrutiny gets less space than the vivid item that will not. A vivid single-source claim in the BLUF, with the boring corroborated material in an annex.
Anchoring Bias Over-reliance on the first figure or judgement offered, with insufficient adjustment away from it, even when the anchor is arbitrary. An early estimate or a source’s first claim sets the frame, and later reporting is pulled toward it rather than appraised on its own. It compounds with overconfidence: the anchored figure then gets held with more certainty than it earned. A quantity named in the first report of the quarter still shaping the estimate in the third, with nobody re-deriving it.
Availability Heuristic Judging likelihood by how easily instances come to mind rather than by base rates. A recent high-profile event inflates the perceived likelihood of that vector, while quiescent but more probable threats are under-weighted. The threat picture for the coming quarter shaped by last month's incident, with the quiet categories unexamined.
Representativeness Heuristic Judging probability by stereotypical similarity — how closely a case matches the mental image of a category. Attribution by resemblance: tooling or tradecraft that "matches the profile" is read as confirming an actor, without asking how many others would produce the same signature. Also produces the conjunction problem — detail makes a scenario feel likelier than its own components. "This looks like what a state-sponsored intrusion looks like, therefore it is one," with no test of how many other actors would look identical.
Absence of Evidence Fallacy Treating "we found nothing" as proof that nothing exists. A property of the collection, not of the target. The dangerous direction is the one that manufactures confidence: non-cooperation read as concealment, a negative finding made to mean non-existence with no record of the search behind it. "There is no evidence of collection against this sector" written as a finding, with nothing said about what was searched.

Estimation and confidence🔗

Bias What it is How it bites an assessment The example
Overconfidence Bias Systematically over-estimating the accuracy of one's own judgements. Stated confidence outruns the track record, and absolute language ("will", "clearly") appears where the evidence supports "likely". Invisible without a written, scored record of prior judgements. A "high confidence" call delivered verbally, with nothing on file that would ever show whether that analyst's high-confidence judgements are right seven times in ten or four.
Hindsight Bias After an outcome is known, over-estimating how foreseeable it was beforehand — "I knew it all along". Memory of one's prior judgements is rewritten to fit the result. Post-mortems conclude a failure "should have been seen", which discourages an honest account of what was knowable at the time and destroys the track record calibration depends on. A lessons-learned review that produces "we should have connected the dots", where none of the participants wrote a judgement down in advance.
Base-Rate Neglect Ignoring the prior probability of an event in favour of vivid, case-level detail. The denominator is never estimated, so a weak indicator is treated as a strong one. Drives false-positive volume and alert fatigue in detection work. An indicator selected for sensitivity, with nobody asking how much benign traffic matches it.
Insensitivity to Sample Size Drawing strong conclusions from small samples. Using them is not the error; in intelligence they are often all there is. The error is not lowering confidence to match. Two or three incidents become a "campaign"; one or two intrusions become an actor's "characteristic" tradecraft — and exactly where collection is thinnest, which is where confidence should be lowest. "We have seen this TTP twice" generalised into an actor profile, with no n anywhere in the sentence.

Social and group🔗

Bias What it is How it bites an assessment The example
Groupthink (Janis 1972)3 A cohesive group's drive for unanimity overriding a realistic appraisal of alternatives. Convergence without genuine challenge; dissent self-censored or pressured away, and silence read as agreement. The group confirms a shared view instead of testing it. A team that has carried the same picture for two years and can no longer name the item that, if withdrawn, would change it.
Conformity Bias Adopting the group's or a senior voice's position regardless of one's own analysis. Individual-level, and distinct from groupthink. Invisible in the product, because the suppressed reasoning is never written down. It looks like deference to authority, which is legitimate almost everywhere else in the organisation and therefore hard to challenge here. An analyst who holds a different reading, hears the room, adjusts, and produces no record that the doubt ever existed.
Fundamental Attribution Error Explaining others’ behaviour by their character while explaining our own by circumstance. Structural pressure becomes disposition: the actor behaves aggressively because they are aggressive. The assessment hardens into a moral characterisation, which is stable, unfalsifiable and useless as an indicator. "Aggressive and opportunistic by nature" as an outlook judgement, with nothing collected on the pressures the actor is actually under.
Loss Aversion Losses loom larger than equivalent gains, skewing risk judgement. Risk judgements turn asymmetric: understating a threat is a career event and overstating it rarely is, so assessments drift upwards under scrutiny. The same asymmetry shapes disclosure decisions. Every ambiguous item read the alarming way, justified by "the consequences of missing this are unacceptable".

Decision and commitment🔗

Bias What it is How it bites an assessment The example
Escalation of Commitment Persisting with a failing line, product or programme because of what has already been spent on it. The sunk-cost fallacy, applied to analysis. A programme continues for its cost rather than its yield. An analytic line is defended past the evidence because dropping it would mean conceding the earlier judgement was wrong. A detection capability that stopped producing eighteen months ago, retained because of what building it cost.
Framing Effect Reaching different conclusions from identical information because of how the question or options were expressed. The customer’s framing is the strongest signal in the tasking and the part least likely to be questioned. That is how a product can be accurate and still wrong: gain and loss framings of the same risk produce different recommendations. A tasking that asks "how serious is this threat?", answered precisely as asked, when "is there evidence of harm?" would have produced a different assessment from the same file.
Status Quo Bias Preferring the existing state, and making change justify itself. A default is a decision nobody argued for. Standing judgements escape scrutiny precisely because no change is being claimed, but "no change" is itself a judgement and needs the same evidence as any other. An assessment never revisited is indistinguishable from one re-confirmed. "Our assessment is unchanged" for the seventh cycle, with no date on when the evidence behind it was last re-tested.
Recency Bias Over-weighting the most recent reporting at the expense of the longer record. The product becomes a summary of the last seven days rather than an assessment of the subject; trends are claimed from two adjacent points with the longer series unexamined. In daily-cycle work it produces oscillation without new information. Reporting volume rising for six weeks, read as escalation, when the rise is a collection decision rather than a change on the ground.

Two notes before we use it.

Which rows need somebody else’s agreement. Most cognitive rows are yours to catch in a draft: confirmation, vividness, anchoring, overconfidence, absence of evidence. Four are not. Groupthink and Conformity Bias are behaviour of a group, and Loss Aversion and Escalation of Commitment are written into how the organisation and the programme are managed. The five structural rows are not yours at all. If the check sheet only carries rows you can run alone, it will pass drafts that are systematically wrong.

Which rows are one mechanism wearing two names. Availability Heuristic and Recency Bias. Representativeness Heuristic and Base-Rate Neglect. Overconfidence Bias and Insensitivity to Sample Size. Do not collect the whole set in one draft. Pick the mechanism that is operating and say what produced it. A hunt that finds ten biases has usually found one bias and nine labels.

Structural bias — in the system🔗

Five patterns. None is a defect in anybody’s reasoning; they are properties of the posture: who collected it, how many people read it, what the partner sent, whether anyone scheduled a re-test, whether volume was ever separated from activity. The tell shows up in the product just as a cognitive one does. The fix does not, because it belongs to somebody with authority over the team or the collection. That is why the last column is a role.

Pattern What it is How it bites an assessment Who owns the fix
Single-Source Dependency The key judgement rests on one report, through a chain the analyst cannot inspect to the end. The vividness and detail of the single report substitute for corroboration, and the product never says what would happen to the judgement if that source were wrong. Collection manager
Inherited Partner Reporting Material from another service, arriving without the grading and handling caveats that sit behind it. The partner's confidence language is re-published as fact; the caveats are lost at the boundary; the customer cannot see that two links in the chain are outside our control. Liaison owner
Thin Analytic Coverage One analyst, or two, on a question of national significance, with no independent second reading. Every cognitive bias on this page depends on a second reader to catch it. Where coverage is thin, none of them are catchable, and the gap is invisible in the product. Team lead
Carried-Forward Assessment A standing judgement that survives because nothing in the cycle requires it to be tested. "No change" travels forward without a decision, so the judgement can outlive the evidence that produced it — and nobody can name the date it was last tested. Product owner
Volume Mistaken for Development Reporting volume, which is a property of the collection system, read as activity on the ground. A new feed or a re-tasked sensor looks like escalation; the trend is a statement about us, published as a statement about them. Collection manager

Two of these overlap deliberately with cognitive rows. Carried-Forward Assessment is the process version of Status Quo Bias, and Volume Mistaken for Development is the posture version of Recency Bias. Naming the right one matters, because they need different people in the room. The clip is also the reason carried-forward assessment is dangerous: change is easy to miss while you are looking straight at it.

Exercise 1 — the hunt (25 minutes)🔗

Work in pairs. For each numbered judgement, name the bias and say what in the text produced it. The second half is the part that counts. A bias with no tell is a bias you have not found.

One test applies throughout, because the rows sit close together: ask what the neighbouring bias would have looked like instead. If you cannot say what separates representativeness from confirmation in this text, you have matched a word rather than read the mechanism.

The draft. Written for this exercise. It is not a real assessment, it is not about a real actor, and every judgement in it is invented. It is deliberately overloaded: a real draft this length would carry two or three of these, not nineteen. The compression is the point. You are learning the tells, not auditing a plausible product.

Tasking. How serious is the threat to critical infrastructure from state-linked intrusion activity this quarter?

BLUF. A state-linked group is conducting a sustained campaign against Australian critical-infrastructure providers. We assess with high confidence that it intends to preposition access for disruptive operations.

Key judgements

  1. We assess with high confidence that the group is Vantage. Its tooling and its targeting closely match Vantage's established profile.
  2. We have observed this signature twice this quarter and assess it as Vantage's characteristic tradecraft.
  3. There is no evidence of collection against the water sector, which indicates the group has not yet moved against that target set.
  4. Reporting volume has risen every week since August, and the campaign will clearly escalate.
  5. Our assessment is unchanged from the previous six products.

Basis. The assessment rests on reporting from a partner service, which does not permit direct access to the underlying collection. One report describes the tooling in detail and carries judgement 1. Where reporting is ambiguous we have taken the more alarming reading, since a missed warning is the more serious error. Public reporting on the group's most recent victim, a regional hospital, corroborates the picture.

Outlook. We have consistently assessed that the group will prioritise energy over water. Its restraint toward the water sector is difficult to reconcile with the behaviour we would expect from an actor seeking maximum effect. The group is aggressive and opportunistic by nature.

Handling. A colleague questioned the attribution in review. The query did not change the assessment and has not been recorded.

Your task, before opening the reveal:

  1. For every numbered judgement, plus the tasking, the basis, the outlook and the handling paragraph, name the bias and the phrase in the text that produces it. Expect eighteen to twenty rows.
  2. Name the judgement that would collapse the BLUF if it were withdrawn.
  3. Name the biases in this draft that no individual analyst can fix alone, including any that belong to the collection rather than to the writing.

The reveal runs in five blocks, in the order you would work them. Open each when both of you have committed an answer.

Reveal 1 of 5 — the frame the customer set

This mapping is a reading of a constructed draft, written for training. It is not a finding about any real assessment.

What the draft says The bias Why that one, and not the one next to it
Tasking: "How serious is the threat…?" Framing Effect The question is framed as a severity judgement, so the file is read for severity. Framed as "is there evidence of harm?", the same material could produce a notably lower assessment. The tell is that the framing is the customer's, and nobody rewrote it.
Reveal 2 of 5 — the BLUF and the five key judgements

Ten rows in a dozen sentences. This is the block worth arguing about.

What the draft says The bias Why that one, and not the one next to it
"a sustained campaign" in the BLUF Insensitivity to Sample Size Two observations are described as a campaign. Not base-rate neglect — the error here is the n, not a missing denominator. The honest sentence names the count: "activity observed on two occasions".
"high confidence" throughout Overconfidence Bias Confidence language is asserted and never earned. It is not vividness — nothing is vivid about the phrase; the failure is calibration. The tell is that nothing in the draft could ever show whether this analyst's high-confidence calls are right most of the time.
KJ1 — "tooling and targeting closely match Vantage's established profile" Representativeness Heuristic Attribution by resemblance. Distinguish it from confirmation: confirmation would be discounting a rival; this is never forming one. The test the draft fails is "how many other actors would look exactly like this?"
KJ1, and the absence of any rival Confirmation Bias No competing hypothesis appears anywhere in the draft, so nothing could ever be tested against the leading one. The tell is the structure rather than the sentence: a single hypothesis and an accumulating pile of support.
KJ2 — "observed this signature twice… characteristic tradecraft" Insensitivity to Sample Size The same mechanism as the campaign line, applied to a TTP. One or two intrusions become a profile. Not representativeness: nobody is matching a stereotype here; the count is simply too small to carry the word "characteristic".
KJ3 — "no evidence of collection against water, which indicates it has not yet moved" Absence of Evidence Fallacy A negative finding about someone else's collection is written as a positive finding about the target. Not confirmation bias on its own — the mechanism is the specific inversion of absent evidence into evidence of absence. The fix is a sentence about what was searched.
KJ4 — "volume has risen every week since August, and the campaign will clearly escalate" Recency Bias The recent period supplies the trend and the longer record is not consulted. The volume rise may be a collection decision rather than anything on the ground.
KJ4, "will clearly escalate" Overconfidence Bias (again), with Base-Rate Neglect "Will clearly" is a certainty word standing where the evidence supports "likely" — and no prior for how often such activity escalates was ever stated. Two mechanisms, one sentence; say which one you are calling.
KJ5 — "unchanged from the previous six products" Status Quo Bias, then Belief Perseverance The default is carried forward and treated as requiring no evidence. Where a judgement has survived six products, the harder question is whether it would survive the withdrawal of the sources that first produced it — that is belief perseverance, and it is why Workshop 02's "layering effect" belongs here, and the Senate committee named it as a failure of analytic tradecraft rather than of collection.4
KJ5 again, read as a programme Escalation of Commitment Six products behind the line is an investment, and abandoning it now would concede the earlier ones were wrong. Note it is the same sentence as the row above and a different mechanism — which is exactly why the exercise asks for the phrase that produces the bias.
Reveal 3 of 5 — the basis, where the shape of the collection shows

This block produces the free finding: the structural row carrying the whole product.

What the draft says The bias Why that one, and not the one next to it
Basis: "rests on reporting from a partner service, which does not permit direct access… one report carries judgement 1" Structural single-source dependency, with Vividness Bias Not a cognitive bias at all: it is a property of the collection. A single, detailed, vivid report behind the key judgement, with no direct access at either end of the chain. Butler's point from Workshop 02 applies — one source is not automatically weak, but a claim about an actor's identity resting on one indirect report is a different object.
Basis: "public reporting on the group's most recent victim, a regional hospital, corroborates the picture" Vividness Bias, with Availability Heuristic The dramatic case feels like corroboration and is not: a victim report cannot corroborate an attribution by tooling. Vividness explains why it was counted; availability explains why it came to mind.
Basis: "where reporting is ambiguous we have taken the more alarming reading, since a missed warning is the more serious error" Loss Aversion Stated outright as a policy, which is what makes this row so useful: the asymmetry of institutional risk has been written into the method. The mirror of it is that an unnecessary warning is treated as costing nothing.
Reveal 4 of 5 — the outlook and the handling

Five rows across two paragraphs, two of which belong to the team rather than to the sentence.

What the draft says The bias Why that one, and not the one next to it
Outlook: "we have consistently assessed that the group will prioritise energy over water" Anchoring Bias The original call is the anchor, and every later product has adjusted around it rather than re-derived it.
Outlook: "its restraint toward water is difficult to reconcile with the behaviour we would expect from an actor seeking maximum effect" Mirror Imaging The standard of rationality applied is ours. This is the sentence to sit with: the assumption that a rational actor maximises effect, unexamined, is doing the work of an entire collection plan.
Outlook: "aggressive and opportunistic by nature" Fundamental Attribution Error Conduct becomes character. Distinguish from mirror imaging: mirror imaging attributes our reasoning to them, this attributes no reasoning to them at all. The tell is that the words are unfalsifiable — no collection could prove a nature.
Handling: "a colleague questioned the attribution… did not change the assessment and has not been recorded" Conformity Bias Dissent that existed and was not carried. The mechanism is invisible in the product precisely because it was never written down — which is the argument for recording independent judgements before the discussion.
Handling: the same sentence, read at team level Groupthink The team's shared picture absorbed the query without engaging it. Distinguish from conformity: conformity is the individual suppressing a doubt they can see; groupthink is the group's process making that suppression cost-free and invisible.
Reveal 5 of 5 — the two the draft cannot show, and the answers to Q2 and Q3

Two the draft cannot show.

  • Selective Perception cannot be diagnosed from a single draft. Its tell is an absence: the disconfirming report that was never registered and so never appears in the text. You catch it by looking at the file the draft came from and asking what should have been there. The clip is that absence at full size: 21 changes in a scene everyone was watching.
  • Hindsight Bias cannot appear in a draft written before the outcome. You will meet it in the post-mortem, when this draft’s wrong call becomes obvious in hindsight and nobody can remember how uncertain it felt. Workshop 02 closes on the same warning.

Q2, the judgement the BLUF rests on. Judgement 1. Withdraw the attribution to Vantage and the BLUF loses the group and the intent. Judgement 1 rests on one indirect report from a service that will not permit direct access. That is the free finding: the attribution may well be right, but a single-source dependency is carrying the whole product and nothing in the draft says so.

Q3, the biases no individual can fix alone. Groupthink and Conformity Bias, because both are properties of how the team argues. Loss Aversion and Escalation of Commitment, because both are written into how the organisation and the programme are managed. Every structural row. The rest of the card you can run against your own draft in ten minutes: Framing Effect is a tasking you can rewrite, Status Quo Bias is a date you can add, Recency Bias is a baseline you can print. That split is why the card has two blocks.

What good looks like: at least one row where a pair disagrees about the mechanism and can argue both sides from the text, the single-source dependency named before the reveal, and nobody claiming all nineteen.

Exercise 2 — your own draft (10 minutes)🔗

Bring something you wrote: the last product, a draft in progress, or the assessment you are least comfortable with. Work alone, in writing. Do not edit anything yet.

Four steps, in order.

  1. Write down, on a separate line, the confidence term your BLUF carries and the single item that would collapse it if it were withdrawn. Most people cannot answer the second part from memory, which is the point.
  2. Read the draft once, hunting only for the tells on the card in Exercise 3. One pass. Do not fix anything. A pass that edits and a pass that reads are different passes.
  3. Name the one mechanism that is operating. One. If you can name five, you have not chosen between them.
  4. Write one sentence: what would have to be true for your judgement to be wrong, and who is looking for it.

Then swap with the pair beside you: read their step 3 and say whether the text supports the bias they named.

Reveal — what this pass usually turns up, and the four symptoms to expect

What this pass usually turns up, roughly in this order:

  • An unstated confidence term. The judgement is in absolute language and nobody can say what would move it. This is the most common finding, and it is Overconfidence Bias in a form the writer does not recognise, because it feels like brevity.
  • A single item carrying the BLUF. A figure, or a quote, or one case. The writer usually knows it is the vivid item and has never asked whether it is the reliable one.
  • An unanswered "no evidence of". The negative finding with no collection record behind it, appearing in a product whose author would never accept that standard from a source.
  • "No change" with no date. The standing assessment carried forward, and neither the writer nor the customer able to say when the evidence behind it was last tested.

Four things the room will say:

Symptom What it means
"I can name the bias in everyone else's draft and only a mild one in mine." Normal, and the reason the exercise is written rather than discussed. Your own draft is read through the model that produced it.
"I found six biases." You have matched words, not mechanisms. Go back to step 3 and choose one, with the phrase that produced it.
"There is no bias in this draft." Possible, and rare. Look for the absence instead: Selective Perception has no sentence to point at.
"The bias is in the tasking, not in my work." Likely true, and worth saying. Framing Effect comes in from outside. It does not excuse it: restating the tasking is the analyst’s job and it costs one line.

What good looks like: a written step 3 naming one mechanism with the phrase that produced it, a step 4 sentence somebody else could act on, and a confidence term in step 1 that was not in the draft as written.

Exercise 3 — the signature card (8 minutes)🔗

The deliverable. One row per bias you can actually catch, in this shape:

Bias The signature — what you will see in your own draft The fix — one move

Two rules. The tell has to be quotable: a phrase, a structure, or an absence you would recognise in a draft. A definition is not a tell. The fix has to be a single move: a line you add, a term you re-earn, a name you write down. Anything that reads "be more sceptical" comes off the card.

Write yours before you open the model. Keep the version you will actually use and cut any row that does not fit your products.

Reveal — the model signature card

Generated from the bias pages, so each tell is written once and cannot drift from the page it belongs to. The first block is yours to close before issue. The second is yours to raise, because the fix belongs to somebody with authority over the team or the collection.

Yours to fix in the draft — 17 rows you can close in ten minutes, alone, before issue.

Bias The signature — what you will see in your own draft The fix — one move
Absence of Evidence Fallacy "No evidence of", "we have not seen", "there is no indication". Pair every negative finding with the collection behind it.
Anchoring Bias A number or a phrase that first appeared in an earlier product, unchanged. Re-derive the figure before reusing it, or say that you have not.
Availability Heuristic The most recent incident shaping a forward-looking judgement. Add the historical baseline next to the recent case.
Base-Rate Neglect An indicator, alert or typology with no benign explanation rate. State the benign rate alongside the malicious one, even approximately.
Belief Perseverance A judgement carried into a re-issue without its original uncertainty. Restate the uncertainty at re-issue, not just at first assessment.
Confirmation Bias A hypothesis and a pile of support, with no rival hypothesis anywhere on the page. Write the strongest competing hypothesis beside the leading one, and mark which evidence discriminates.
Framing Effect The tasking's own words, unexamined, at the top of your draft. Rewrite the tasking in your own words, and say what the other framing would have produced.
Fundamental Attribution Error "Aggressive", "opportunistic", "deceptive by nature". State the situational driver that would produce the same behaviour.
Hindsight Bias A post-mortem conclusion that the outcome was foreseeable. Check it against the judgement that was actually written at the time.
Insensitivity to Sample Size "Campaign", "pattern", "characteristic" with no count in the sentence. Put the count (n) in the sentence and let the reader see it.
Mirror Imaging "They would not do X because it makes no sense." One line naming what the actor would have to believe for the behaviour to make sense to them.
Overconfidence Bias "Will", "clearly", "obviously", "always", or an unqualified number. Re-earn or strike each absolute on the final read.
Recency Bias A trend drawn from the current period alone. Show the current period against the prior baseline.
Representativeness Heuristic "Matches the profile", "consistent with the established pattern". Ask how many other actors would look identical, and name the evidence that would separate them.
Selective Perception Nothing — an absence. The item you cannot find because it was never registered. Read the file, not the draft, and name one item that argues against the judgement.
Status Quo Bias "Unchanged", "no change to the previous assessment". Date the review and say when the evidence was last tested.
Vividness Bias A single striking item — a figure, an image, a quote — near the top. State the source's status against that item, in the product.

Yours to raise with whoever owns the team or the collection — 9 rows no amount of personal discipline will close. The signature is still yours to spot; the fix is somebody else's to make.

Bias The signature What to raise, and with whom
Carried-Forward Assessment "No change" accepted by the process, with no scheduled review and nobody able to say who last tested the evidence. Put the review on a calendar with a named owner, and print the date the evidence was last tested.
Conformity Bias A review comment that changed nothing and was not recorded. Record the reasoning before the discussion, and record the query after it.
Escalation of Commitment A line or a capability defended by its history rather than its yield. Ask: if it did not exist today, would we build it?
Groupthink A judgement nobody has argued against, in a team that agrees. Independent judgements in writing, then reconcile; name a challenger.
Inherited Partner Reporting Reporting you did not collect, cannot re-derive, and have never seen the handling caveats behind. State the partner's caveats in your own product in your own words — or state that you do not have them.
Loss Aversion "The consequences of missing this are unacceptable." State what it costs to be wrong in each direction.
Single-Source Dependency The key judgement rests on one report, one chain, and a source you cannot see to the end. Name the dependency in the product, and say what the judgement would look like if that source were wrong.
Thin Analytic Coverage One name on a national question, and no second reading of the same file. Say in the product how many analysts the judgement rests on, and what a second reader would have been asked to check.
Volume Mistaken for Development The reporting curve is rising and the product reads it as the activity curve. Separate the reporting curve from the activity curve, and say which one the trend rests on.

How to use it. The card is a reading aid, not a checklist. Run it once against a draft in the last ten minutes before issue. Work it row by row, looking for something to write in every row, and you will produce the over-calling this session opened by warning against. Expect one or two live entries in a normal draft. Nothing in the second block is a fix you make in a draft; those are conditions you raise with whoever owns the team and the collection.

Reveal — the map: bias → technique → artefact

The card tells you what to look for. The map tells you what to run, and what it leaves behind. Every row of the card is backed by one of the Structured Analytic Techniques, and a technique that is actually run leaves an artefact a reviewer can inspect years later.

Technique The biases it counters The artefact it leaves behind
Devil's advocacy and red-teaming Conformity Bias, Escalation of Commitment, Framing Effect, Groupthink, Loss Aversion, Thin Analytic Coverage A coverage line naming who reviewed the judgement, and against what; Cost-of-error statement in both directions; Independent judgements in writing, plus a named challenger; Tasking restated, with what the other framing would have produced; The build-it-today test, on the record; Written dissent register attached to the product
Key Assumptions Check Anchoring Bias, Carried-Forward Assessment, Fundamental Attribution Error, Mirror Imaging, Status Quo Bias A dated re-test line under every standing judgement; Dated review line: when the evidence was last tested; Situational-driver paragraph beside the conduct; Statement of the actor's premise, written from their position; The figure re-derived, with the source it now rests on
Analysis of Competing Hypotheses Belief Perseverance, Confirmation Bias, Representativeness Heuristic, Selective Perception ACH matrix with the discriminating evidence marked; Evidence file annotated against every hypothesis on the page; List of other actors that would leave the same signature; Re-issued judgement carrying its original confidence term
Baseline comparison Availability Heuristic, Base-Rate Neglect, Recency Bias, Volume Mistaken for Development Baseline series printed beside the recent case; Benign rate stated alongside the malicious one; Current period against the prior baseline; Two labelled series — reporting volume, and activity on the ground
Confidence ledger Hindsight Bias, Insensitivity to Sample Size, Overconfidence Bias Scored record of prior judgements and how they resolved; The judgement as written at the time, quoted in the post-mortem; n stated in the judgement, with confidence set to match
Source grading Inherited Partner Reporting, Single-Source Dependency, Vividness Bias A provenance line naming the originating service and its stated caveats; A source-dependency line under the key judgement; Source status printed against the vivid claim
Collection-gap review Absence of Evidence Fallacy Search record appended to every negative finding

Why the artefact matters more than the technique. From the outside, a technique you ran and cannot show looks the same as one you did not run. The ACH matrix, the assumptions log, the confidence ledger and the dated review line are what separate an analysis that was checked from an analysis that says it was. This is also the page to take to a manager. Each row names a capability, and the empty artefact column is where the team does not have one.

Two artefacts are the exception. A collection record and a coverage line cannot be produced by an analyst working alone; they need the collection manager and the team lead. Treat a blank in those columns as a question for somebody else, which is what the second block of the card says.

What good looks like: every tell quotable, every fix a single move, and at least one row in the second block that you have raised with its owner rather than written down.

Watch for these in your own reasoning🔗

  • Calling bias where there is only disagreement. Quote the phrase that produced it, or you have a label rather than a mechanism.
  • Collecting the whole atlas in one draft. The tells overlap, and the adjacent pairs mostly cannot both operate on the same sentence. Name the one that is.
  • Treating this as a knowledge test. Naming Anchoring Bias correctly has never changed an assessment. Adding one line about the baseline has.
  • Fixing the cognitive rows and ignoring the structural ones. The rows nobody can catch alone carried the Iraq judgements, and they will carry yours. They need a decision by somebody with authority, which means saying them out loud to that person.
  • Learning the atlas from the post-mortem. Hindsight Bias will supply you with a satisfying explanation of every failure you review, and it will be wrong at the margins every time. The only honest test of your judgement is the one you wrote down in advance.

Questions to keep on the table🔗

  • What is the one mechanism operating in your current draft, and what phrase produced it?
  • Which single item would collapse your key judgement if it were withdrawn, and does the customer know it is there?
  • Where in your file is the disconfirming item that never made it into the product, and who is looking for it?
  • Which of your standing judgements has not been re-tested since it was first made, and what would re-testing cost?
  • Which bias have you named in someone else's work this month but never found in your own?
  • What would you have to see for today’s assessment to be wrong, and is anybody collecting it?
