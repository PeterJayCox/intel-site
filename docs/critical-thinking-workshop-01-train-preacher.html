Workshop 01 — Single-source video: a transit encounter

workshopcourseanalysisbiassource-evaluation
Open Source

Format. Facilitated, 60 minutes, one team, discussion throughout. Material. One public six-minute video, with a second upload from the same visit held beside it, plus a bounded open-source check. Output. A statement of the decision the customer faces; a claim-by-claim source table; three prioritised gaps; one page of assessment that a colleague has challenged. Extensions. The legal frame and the staged-testimony problem sit at the end of this page as optional follow-up. They are not part of the hour.

Scope, audience and outcomes

This is an analyst-tradecraft session rather than a general class in critical thinking. It assumes no prior study of intelligence method, but it does assume participants will be asked to produce work in the language of the trade: source grading, estimative confidence, collection gaps.

Three outcomes, and only three, for the 60 minutes.

  1. Separate observation, reported claim, inference and unknown, and say which is which.
  2. Judge how independent a source is, and what that does to confidence.
  3. Turn an under-specified task into a short list of gaps ranked by whether closing them would change the judgement.

Reaching any particular verdict about the man in the clip is not one of the outcomes. Participants are assessed on how they trace a claim to its evidence, how they handle the alternatives, and how they calibrate confidence. A team that declines to reach the conclusion in the model answers, and can say why, has done the exercise well.

Content note and ground rules

  • Subject matter. Street preaching, religion, abortion, and a hostile exchange between strangers on public transport. Some participants will find it grating and a few may find it close to home. The session uses that discomfort as material. It does not debate the beliefs on either side.
  • Opt-out. Anyone who would rather not watch can work from the transcript timeline further down the page and rejoin at Exercise 1. Every exercise below works from the transcript alone, and is arguably harder that way.
  • Boundaries for the open-source check. Search only what is overt, public and lawful: the clip, the publisher's other uploads, published reporting about the incident, the legislation cited in Extension A. Do not look for anyone's home address, workplace, family, associations or contact details, and do not make contact with anyone involved. Nothing found goes into a record outside the session.
  • What is being assessed. Conduct, frequency and harm. Belief, expression and private life sit outside it. If a line of enquiry only tells you what someone thinks, it is out of scope.

Bottom line up front

A customer asks whether the man in the clip is a threat to the public. The material available cannot answer that: the only substantive evidence is one six-minute video, published by the subject's own ministry. So the hour goes on establishing what the question actually requires, testing how far the source can carry us, and writing down what we would need in order to answer it properly. The deliverable is that list of gaps, plus a one-page assessment that states its confidence and its limits.

Two failure modes do most of the work. Framing: whether "threat" is the right question, and which direction the risk runs. Source integrity: the clip is a party to the event publishing its own conduct, which constrains anything built on it.

The customer's question, as given

You work for the NSW Police. You have been asked to assess the threat posed by this individual to the public.

Exercise 3 takes this question apart. Do not answer it before then.

What the transcript did to an error

At 1:11 a passenger says bus. It was a train: the speaker said so at 0:24, the clip's own title says so, and every subsequent report says so. The interesting part is what happened next.

The machine transcript carried the slip through verbatim. The platform's auto-chapter then titled that stretch of video Tensions arise on the bus. A slip of the tongue became a description of the setting, through two machine-generated layers, with nobody deciding that it should. The transcript corrected nothing, because a transcript cannot.

This is why the text record is treated as a separate source with its own failure modes, and why Exercise 2 grades it separately from the video.

Exercise 1 — Claim status (8 minutes)

Work the material on the board under four statuses. Every line anyone writes down gets one of them.

  • Observation. Shown in the clip or on the platform record. Anyone watching would see the same thing.
  • Reported. Asserted by a party to the event, or by a document, with no independent confirmation available to us.
  • Inference. Not shown, but implied by what is shown. An inference carries a confidence level and at least one rival.
  • Unknown. The material cannot settle it.

Build your own table first. Then open the model answer and compare statuses rather than wording.

The exercise also has a use for the title. Ask once, of any line in the table: is that a fact, or is it the title? The word backlash comes from the publisher's caption, not from anything the clip establishes about how the encounter was received.

The inference this material does support

One inference is available, and it is worth working properly because it is the model for the rest.

The camera settles on the man, follows him, and holds him in frame at conversational distance while he moves through the carriage and turns to different passengers. He is addressing the passengers rather than the lens. Nobody visible is holding a camera.

A second person was therefore present at the moment of the encounter, filming deliberately. Confidence in that much is high: the framing has to come from somewhere, and the speaker's hands are occupied. What that second person's role was is a separate question, and lower confidence.

Rival explanation How it rates
A companion camera operator, filming for the ministry Leading. It fits the framing, the follow-the-subject movement and the distance.
A passenger filmed it, and the ministry obtained the footage later Possible. On screen it would look identical. The evidence that would separate it is the same footage appearing on another channel first, or a credit or thank-you post.
The subject filmed himself, hand-held or on a rig Weak. The frame is stable and set back, it moves with the crowd, and his hands are occupied.

The evidence that would separate these: whether the camera follows him off the carriage or between services; whether other uploads from the same channel show the same framing hand; whether a voice near the microphone belongs to the operator; whether the ministry ever describes a team or credits a videographer.

One item on that list is now partly answered. In the sibling upload from the same visit, a second voice is audible at 6:20 — "that's some fire preacher right there" — while the speaker is mid-gesture with both hands occupied, so somebody else was holding that camera. It proves nothing about this carriage. It is the cross-upload check the list is asking for, and it is how the item moves from open to partly held.

A team that rates the second-person finding lower than high, and says which rival it takes seriously, is making a better analytic move than one that simply adopts the model answer. Say what confidence you attach and what would move it.

Push on this: what is the weakest status assignment on the board, and who can argue it down?

Exercise 2 — Source relationships and limits (10 minutes)

Part A — what supports each claim

Six questions are usually compressed into one letter grade. Keep them apart.

  • Provenance. Who produced this, and what is their relationship to the event?
  • Authenticity. Is the artefact what it purports to be?
  • Completeness. What was excluded, and can we tell?
  • Representativeness. Is this instance typical, or selected?
  • Independence. Does it derive from the same origin as everything else we hold?
  • Currency. Does it describe a present condition or a past one?

Three consequences follow, and they are the ones to reach in discussion.

A source that is also the sole evidence gets flagged as single-source, and the confidence of anything built on it is capped accordingly. That is a statement about the strength of the evidence, not a claim that no other evidence exists anywhere. Those are different claims and only one of them is testable in the next five minutes.

The transcript is a separate artefact with its own failure modes. No punctuation, profanity redacted, no speaker attribution, and a preserved error where a correction was needed (the bus at 1:11, carried into the auto-chapter as a description of the setting). It caps what can be quoted from the clip. It does not, on its own, make the video less authentic.

The age of the material cuts both ways. A seven-year-old clip is poor evidence of what someone is doing now, and perfectly good evidence of what the published video shows. Keep the two questions apart or the grade will hide the difference.

So the accurate call is not "no second source". It is this: one recording of the encounter has been located; the reporting is independent of the publisher but derivative of that recording; and a participant account is reported to exist, its contents unreviewed. Absence claims take the form "we did not find it in this search", and they carry the search's limits with them.

When someone reports an identification

Someone may arrive having watched both of the uploads held, and report that a face in one is a face in the other. Treat it as a report, like any other, and apply the same questions.

  • Record the basis, not the conclusion. "A viewer who watched both uploads reports the same man in each" is a reported claim from an identified source. "The same man appears in both" is an observation, and nobody has made it.
  • Check the resolution before the claim. The frames that would support such a report can be about 60 by 95 pixels in the source. At that size clothing can be compared and a face cannot. A match on a white top and a shoulder strap is a resemblance, and a resemblance is not an identification.
  • Ask what would settle it. A frame with a legible face in both uploads; the publisher naming or crediting him; a second camera. Until one of those exists, it stays a report with its confidence capped and its limit stated.
  • Keep it out of the register either way. A row would make a private individual the subject of collection, and the question it answers, who he is, moves nothing in the customer's decision.
  • Say the limit out loud. The point is not to solve the identification. It is to notice how much easier it is to accept a match you want than to state what the match rests on.

If your house method requires a single Admiralty grade, two are defensible, and the reasoning is worth more than the letters.

Grading The argument for it
B2 — usually reliable, probably true The footage is authentic material of a real encounter rather than testimony about one, and the publisher is a competent reporter of its own activity.
C3 — fairly reliable, possibly true The material is single-source and self-published, speaker attribution is absent from the transcript, editorial selection is unverifiable, and the encounter is seven years old.

Both are arguable because the letter fuses questions that the table above keeps apart: authenticity, completeness, representativeness, independence and currency all get folded into one judgement. Reach a call, note which question did the work, and keep the fuse short.

What the source cannot tell us: who else was involved; who any of the people in it are, since the transcript attributes no line and the camera stays behind its subject; what happened before or after the clip; whether the encounter was typical of anything; whether the subject is still active, still in Australia, or still practising; whether anyone complained, was charged or was hurt; and whether any of it has current relevance at all.

Metrics. The view count describes distribution. It does not describe threat, and it does not describe the audience's agreement. Two uploads from the same visit turn the incentive argument from a hypothesis into a measurement: the confrontation-framed clip sits at 717,468 viewings and the sermon-framed one at 49,020, roughly fifteen to one, from one channel in one week. What moved was the framing, not the message.

Push on this: which of the six source questions has the most influence on your confidence, and would a different team have chosen a different one?

Exercise 3 — Reframe the task (10 minutes)

Interrogate the question before anyone collects a single item.

  • What decision does the customer actually need to make?
  • Who or what is being protected?
  • What is the time horizon: tonight, this month, or is this historical?
  • What threshold does the customer mean: offensive conduct, unlawful conduct, or physical violence?
  • What would "no threat" look like, and what would it take to say it?
  • Is the material current?
Model answer — the assumptions inside the question, five framings, and the risk to officers

Two assumptions sit inside the task as given. That the preacher is the threat source, and that a 2019 clip is evidence of a present condition. Both are testable, and testing them is the exercise.

Five framings are available, and each implies different collection, a different product and different risks.

Framing The question it implies What it would require
H1 — the speaker is the threat source Public order, offensive conduct, harassment of a captive audience Complaint records, transport operator logs, legal review of the conduct offence
H2 — the speaker is the one at risk An identifiable and contrarian speaker, filmed in an enclosed space, facing a hostile crowd Assault and incident data, counter-mobilisation, crowd behaviour in comparable settings
H3 — the clip is the vector Content distributed for engagement, with the encounter incidental to the distribution Platform analytics, the rest of the channel, whether the format repeats
H4 — currency failure A seven-year-old artefact unless the subject is active in NSW now Presence, activity and recency, collected before anything else
H5 — the risk is to attending officers Police attend a polarising speaker in an enclosed carriage with an agitated crowd and a camera running Officer safety in a confined space, crowd dynamics, and what the footage becomes afterwards

The direction of risk and the currency of the material do most of the work. A team that settles on H1 by default has answered a question nobody asked, and may have written a record of lawful expression while believing it wrote a threat assessment.

Note also what the task does not define. "Threat" without a threshold can mean conduct that offends, conduct that is unlawful, or conduct that injures. Until the customer picks one, the product has no test to pass.

The risk to attending officers

The customer is the police, and the police are also exposed by this scenario. Nothing in the clip shows hostility towards police. The exposure is situational, which is why it is easy to miss.

  • Physical. A confined carriage, a hostile crowd, and an arrival to enforce an outcome. The crowd's anger transfers to whoever represents authority, and officers would be working shoulder to shoulder with passengers who are already shouting at a stranger.
  • The camera changes the calculus. The publisher's pattern, so far as one channel shows it, is to film encounters and publish them. That is a hypothesis drawn from the material and worth testing. If it holds, then any police attendance becomes content, and what circulates is the edited and titled product in place of the officer's account. A lawful and proportionate intervention can still be presented otherwise, and the intervention itself can become the story.
  • Legal and reputational. An intervention against a minority-position speaker, filmed and titled for engagement, is a reputational event whatever its merits, and is reusable afterwards by political actors.

For an officer on the day, the tactical option is also a communications decision. That is a real finding for a police customer, and it is not reachable by asking whether the man is dangerous.

The legal frame for the conduct in the clip is held in Extension A at the end of this page, which is optional follow-up rather than part of the hour.

Push on this: write the reframed task in one sentence that names the customer's decision, the time horizon and the direction of risk. Nothing moves to collection until the sentence exists.

The priority rule

Gaps that would change the judgement come first. Gaps that only add colour are not gaps. The view count, the speaker's nationality, his denomination and his theology are all easy to obtain, all interesting, and none of them move the assessment. A register padded with them has not been thought about, and ten minutes does not produce a dozen rows. Three that would change the call beat nine that restate the discussion.

One row here exists only because of Exercise 1, where the inference about the camera produced the second-person finding. Infer, then collect.

What not to collect

  • Anything about the individual beyond overt, public and lawful material, unless a real tasking, authority and legal review exist. The scenario grants none of them and the exercise does not need them.
  • A profile of anyone's beliefs, associations or expression in place of evidence of conduct. Belief is not a threat indicator. Conduct, frequency and harm are.
  • Platform metrics as though they were intelligence. They are the publisher's distribution figures.

Push on this: which single gap, if filled, would most change your judgement, and who is prepared to say the other two are less important?

Exercise 5 — Draft and peer challenge (7 minutes)

Draft one page, then hand it to a colleague whose job is to attack it. The challenger takes one of three roles and argues it hard.

  • Source sceptic. Show me the claim you cannot trace to the material.
  • Alternative advocate. Give me the H2 reading, or the H3 one, as if you believed it.
  • Customer. Tell me what I do differently tomorrow, and what you would need to tell me that.

The draft is a template exercise and stays in the session. Confidence language comes from Estimative Language and the shape follows Bottom Line Up Front.

Model answer — the worked example, and the peer challenge it invites

This is a template, not an assessment. It demonstrates form: the reframed question, explicit confidence, the separation of fact from judgement, and the gaps left open. Nothing here may be lifted into a real product without the collection, authorities and handling a real product requires.

Task as reframed. Does the subject present a current public-safety or public-order risk to NSW transit users, and if so in which direction?

BLUF. The material available cannot answer the question as posed. The only substantive source is a single first-party upload from 2019. We hold high confidence that the source is inadequate for a judgement about the subject, and high confidence that nothing in the material evidences a present condition, noting that this reflects an empty evidence base rather than a finding of safety.

Key judgements.

  • Currency: no finding possible. Nothing gathered establishes whether the subject is active in NSW today. Absence of evidence is a collection gap, not evidence of absence. Confidence: n/a.
  • Source: single, interested, edited. The clip is authentic as an artefact and unusable as the sole basis for a judgement. Confidence: high.
  • Risk direction: unresolved. If the observed pattern were current, the risk would run in both directions, from a disruptive approach in an enclosed space towards the public, and from an agitated crowd towards the speaker. Confidence: low about this subject, moderate as a statement about the activity in general.
  • Intent and character: no judgement offered. No lawful collection supports one, and none is needed for the decision the customer faces.

What would change the call. A current incident report from the operator; evidence of repeated practice on NSW services; a complaint history in either direction; an assault at either end of an encounter; evidence of organised activity; or one independent recording of the same encounter.

Recommendation. Do not accept the task as framed. Establish the decision it serves. If the concern is transit disruption, the operator's incident data answers it. If the concern is speech that offends, that is a legal and policy question rather than an intelligence one, and framing it as a threat assessment creates a record that will not bear the weight.

Caveats. Judgements about the source carry high confidence. Judgements about the subject carry low confidence and rest on a single-source base with no independent corroboration. Outstanding collection gaps: currency, pattern, complaint history.

Three challenges a colleague should raise against that draft: that "no finding possible" on currency is stated with more certainty than an unreviewed search supports; that the recommendation has strayed from assessment into policy advice; and that the risk-direction judgement names a pattern the material does not show. Take the challenges, then decide which ones the draft survives.

Push on this: which sentence in your draft would you delete if a lawyer read it?

Debrief — 5 minutes

Return to the judgement each participant wrote at 04–10, before any discussion. Three questions, taken in turn.

  1. What changed? Name the single most influential thing you heard or checked.
  2. What is still open? Name one gap you would still want filled.
  3. What transfers? Name one habit from today that would have changed a product you wrote in the last month.

Two failure modes ran through the session, and they are the two to name in the debrief. Framing: a question accepted without examination produces a product nobody asked for. Source integrity: reliable material can still be a single interested source, and that caps what can be built on it.

The hour's working commitments, for anyone who wants them in one place.

  • Claims carry a status, including the ones you are sure about.
  • Independence is a separate question from reliability, and it is the one that caps confidence.
  • Gaps that would change the judgement come first, and a short register beats a long one.
  • Confirmation language is part of the product, and so is the statement of what would change it.

Assessed on the reasoning, not the conclusion

The model answers on this page are one defensible reading. They are not the pass mark. Participants are assessed on the moves below, and a team that reaches a different conclusion while making these moves has done better than one that reproduces the answers.

Biases this material invites

Bias Where it bites here
Vividness Bias and the Availability Heuristic Six minutes of raised voices followed by a view counter. Both make the incident feel significant because it is memorable. Neither bears on whether it matters.
Anchoring Bias The title's "fierce backlash" sets the frame before anyone has heard a word of the clip.
Mirror Imaging Reading a US street evangelist against Sydney commuter norms, and assuming he reads the carriage the way we would.
Confirmation Bias Once somebody suspects the testimony was staged, the rest of the clip begins to look staged. Extension B is where that suspicion gets named, given what supports it, and parked.
Groupthink The pull towards "nuisance" inside three minutes. This is what the private written judgement at 04–10 exists to interrupt.
Hindsight Bias By the end of the debrief the answers feel obvious. They were not obvious at minute ten.

Watch for these in your own reasoning

  • Moralising. The material is built to provoke. Convert every value judgement into an evidentiary one: what would we need in order to know that?
  • Converging early. "He is a nuisance" is a defensible reading and it arrives fast. Arriving there without a confidence level, a gap list and a statement of what would change the call is the failure mode. Make yourself produce all three.
  • Metric worship. The view count will be offered as evidence of significance. Ask what decision the count informs.

Questions to keep on the table

  • Who is the customer, and what do they do differently tomorrow if we are right?
  • If a passenger had filmed the same encounter instead of the speaker, what would change in our reading?
  • Which single gap, if filled, would most change the judgement?
  • What does "lawful but disruptive" require of a police customer, and is that an intelligence question at all?
  • What would we write if the subject were a well-known public figure rather than a stranger on a train?

After-action step. Log the judgements made in the session and revisit them in a few weeks, per the evaluation phase of The Intelligence Cycle. Which were right, which were wrong, and which gaps turned out to be answerable all along.

Extension A — the legal frame

Optional. Fifteen minutes, and not part of the 60-minute run.

This section is a dated reference, not a legal opinion, and it has not been legally reviewed. It was checked against the legislation on 26 September 2026. Read the consolidated Acts rather than a summary, and re-check anything before you rely on it.

The exercise here is checking a premise rather than finding a charge. The premise that needs checking is the common first instinct: that this man can be charged under the safe access zone laws, and that the law therefore settles the question of whether he should have been stopped.

  • Summary Offences Act 1988 (NSW) — s 4 (offensive conduct) and s 4A (offensive language). As checked, s 4 carries a maximum of 6 penalty units or 3 months' imprisonment, and s 4A carries 6 penalty units.
  • Safe access zones — Public Health Act 2010 (NSW) Part 6A, inserted by the Public Health Amendment (Safe Access to Reproductive Health Clinics) Act 2018 (NSW), assented 15 June 2018 and commenced on assent. A safe access zone is the clinic premises and the area within 150 metres of them or of a pedestrian access point. Sections 98C–98E cover interference with access, communications about abortion likely to cause distress or anxiety, and capturing or distributing visual data.
  • The exemption is the instructive part. Section 98F carves out conduct in a church or its curtilage, and the forecourt or footpath outside Parliament House in Macquarie Street. A statute that protects a zone around a clinic while exempting places of worship and the seat of Parliament is a law about access rather than about speech.
  • The premise to check. Those provisions operate inside a safe access zone. A moving train carriage is not one, and the clip contains no clinic. The instinct to reach for the abortion laws is wrong on these facts, and the useful move is noticing that you reached for it.

Hold three distinctions apart. Offensive conduct is not necessarily unlawful. Unlawful conduct is not necessarily a security threat. And a threat assessment is not a charge. Each is a different question with a different test, and merging them produces a record that cannot support the decision it claims to serve.

Verify the statute directly rather than trusting this summary.

  • Summary Offences Act 1988 No 25 — s 4 and s 4A
  • Public Health Act 2010 No 127 — Part 6A (ss 98A–98F), the provisions in force today
  • Public Health Amendment (Safe Access to Reproductive Health Clinics) Act 2018 No 26 — the inserting Act, its assent and its commencement

Extension B — the staged-testimony problem

Optional. Ten minutes, and not part of the 60-minute run.

At 5:20–5:39 a man offers a conversion story that fits the ministry's message closely. It arrives in the closing minute, as the clip turns towards its appeal. A suspicion forms quickly: that the testimony was staged for the camera.

The suspicion is no longer untestable. It is still not resolved. Work both halves.

  • What now supports it. A viewer who has watched both of the uploads held reports the same man in each: in the carriage at 5:25, and in the background of the financial district video published about seventeen hours later. If that holds, the man is not a stranger who happened to speak up on a train. He is still in the ministry's company the following day, on camera, in a video the ministry published. That is more than the clip could offer on its own, and it came from a cross-upload check rather than from the clip.
  • What still limits it. The report rests on a visual comparison whose supporting frames are about 60 by 95 pixels, so the clothing matches and the faces cannot be compared. Nobody has named, credited or described him. And association a day later is not scripting at the time: a genuine convert who spent the trip in the ministry's company looks identical in the evidence. Both readings survive.
  • Unchanged, for the suspicion. The narrative fits the message closely; it arrives where a producer would want it; it is the only sympathetic voice in the clip; and the clip was authored by an interested party.
  • Unchanged, against it. Spontaneous testimony is common in this activity; the man sounds unrehearsed, is interrupted repeatedly and never finishes; a planted confederate would usually be closer to the microphone; and the transcript cannot reliably say who is speaking at any moment.

Three things follow.

First, this concerns a private individual who has not been charged with anything and is not the subject of the session. The report is carried as a report, with its basis and its resolution limit attached, and it goes no further than this page. It is not a name, and it should not become one.

Second, it does not become a collection task. The question it answers — who the man is — moves nothing for the customer, and nothing about it would be collected lawfully.

Third, watch what it does to your reading of the rest of the clip. Once the suspicion lands, the whole encounter starts to look produced. That is the mechanism of Confirmation Bias, and it is why the finding stays out of the gap register even now that it has evidence behind it. Partial support makes a hypothesis better supported, not proven.
