Workshop 01 — the transit clip

workshopcourseanalysissource-evaluation
Open Source

Format. Facilitated, 60 minutes, one team, discussion throughout. Material. One public six-minute video. Output. One completed worksheet: a 5W+H with every answer marked for how it is known, a call on which answers must follow and which only probably follow, and three gaps ranked.

Purpose

Use one video to describe an encounter, distinguish what follows necessarily from what is merely probable, and identify the three most important pieces of missing information.

Working question

What does the clip show about this encounter, and what further information would we need in order to assess its safety implications?

The question is bounded on purpose. We are not diagnosing this man, and we are not assessing a real person's present-day risk from historical footage.

Run sheet — 60 minutes

Time Activity
0–03 Purpose, working question and content note
03–12 Watch the clip, then privately write an initial impression. The clip runs 6:22; the rest of the slot is for the writing
12–25 Exercise 1 — the 5W+H
25–40 Exercise 2 — must follow, or probably follows
40–55 Exercise 3 — gap analysis
55–60 Revisit the initial impression, then debrief

Content note

  • The clip is street preaching, religion, abortion, and a hostile exchange between strangers on a train. We use the discomfort as material, and we do not debate the beliefs on either side.
  • Anyone who would rather not watch can work from the transcript further down and rejoin at Exercise 1.
  • We describe conduct, frequency and harm here. Beliefs and private life are outside the exercise.
  • You will propose checks. You are not investigating the people shown.

Built as a training exercise on a public video. It is not an intelligence product, and nothing on this page is a finding about any person.

Exercise 1 — the 5W+H (13 minutes)

Six rows. Every answer carries one of four statuses.

  • Observed. Shown or heard in the clip, or on the platform record. Anyone watching or listening would experience the same thing.
  • Reported. Asserted by a party to the event, with no independent confirmation available here.
  • Inferred. Not shown, but implied by what is shown. An inference carries a confidence level and at least one rival.
  • Unknown. The material cannot settle it.

Do not fill every box. An explained unknown is better than an invented answer.

Question Answer Evidence and status
Who? Who is involved?
What? What happened?
When? When did it happen?
Where? Where did it happen?
Why? What purpose is stated or inferred?
How? How did the encounter unfold?

The recurring question to ask of every answer: what supports that?

Two things this table shows without being told. Every row rests on a party to the event publishing its own conduct, so the table describes what that party chose to make visible. And the six rows look equally solid whether or not the source supports them, which is the reason for the status column.

Probe: ask once, of any line above, is that a fact or is it the title?

Exercise 2 — must follow, or probably follows (15 minutes)

First, before any definitions. Take one row of your own 5W+H table. Ask of that answer: does it have to follow from what we have, or does it only probably follow?

Then the distinction.

  • Deduction. If the premises are true, the conclusion is guaranteed. Everything rests on the premises.
  • Induction. The premises support the conclusion without guaranteeing it. The risk sits in the reasoning, so an induction always needs the rival it has to beat.

One warning: "general to specific" versus "specific to general" is not a reliable test between them. A deduction can run in either direction. What defines it is the guarantee.

Then a deduction.

Rule: every incident that causes an injury requires review. Fact: this incident caused an injury. Therefore this incident requires review.

Ask three questions. What are the premises? Must the conclusion follow? And which premise is doing the work: has it been established here, or assumed?

Model answer, including two inductions from the clip

The deduction. The conclusion follows necessarily, and that is the least interesting thing about it. The rule is stipulated, so it cannot be false by accident; the fact is supplied, so it is not in dispute. Change either and the conclusion collapses. The useful question is always which premise you are assuming, and a stipulated rule hides that by looking like authority.

Induction 1. A machine transcript carries an error through, and a machine chapter marker then repeats it as a description of the setting.

  • From: at 1:11 a passenger says bus in a train carriage, and the transcript carries the word through. The platform's auto-chapter then titled that stretch of video Tensions arise on the bus.
  • Rival: this may be one clip's accident rather than a property of transcripts. Speech recognition corrects many errors, and the output changes between versions.
  • Confidence: moderate for this transcript, low as a general rule.

Induction 2. Machine-generated metadata can be wrong about the material it describes.

  • From: the platform's first chapter marker is Sharing personal testimony, pinned at 0:00, while the testimony it names begins at 5:20 and the first ten seconds are silent title cards.
  • Rival: the marker may refer to something else, or the chaptering and the transcript may be produced at different times from different audio.
  • Confidence: moderate.

One more answer the clip supports without proving it. The camera holds a steady frame at conversational distance while the man moves through the carriage, turns, and gestures with both hands occupied. Nobody visible is holding a camera, and the clip never shows a camera operator at any point.

  • Inferred: a second person was present at the moment of the encounter.
  • Rivals: a passenger filmed it and the ministry obtained the footage later, which would look identical on screen; the subject filmed himself on a rig.
  • Confidence: high that a second person was present, much lower on what their role was.
  • What would settle it: whether the camera follows him off the carriage; whether other uploads from the same channel show the same framing hand; whether the operator's voice is audible.

That last one is worth pausing on, because the clip cannot show you the thing you are claiming. You are inferring a cause you cannot see from an effect you can. It is a sound inference, and it stays an inference until something outside this clip supports it.

Probe: which of the claims on the board would you be willing to write into a product, and what confidence would you attach?

One selection rule:

An unanswered question becomes a priority gap only if answering it could change the assessment or a relevant decision.

You propose the checks. You do not run them.

Model answer
Gap What would its answer change? How could we check? Rank
What happened before the clip starts and after it ends Whether this was an approach to strangers or a continuation of something, and whether anything escalated or was defused while the camera was off A second recording, or the unedited footage. Nothing lawful is available in this scenario 1
Whether the conduct is current Whether the safety question is live at all, or a matter of record An overt check on whether the group is currently active in Australia 2
Whether anyone complained, was injured, or was charged, in either direction Whether the incident had consequences, and for whom Transport operator records; internal holdings 3

A question that is not a gap. The man's biography, his background and what he believes would add colour and change nothing in this exercise. Under the rule, a question that cannot change the assessment or a decision is not a priority gap, however interesting it is.

On confidence: the first gap is ranked first because the recorded segment is bounded, and escalation is what a bounded recording hides.

Debrief (5 minutes)

Return to the impression each person wrote before the discussion. Then check three things, and nothing else.

  • Can you support your description with evidence?
  • Can you explain the difference between what must follow and what probably follows?
  • Can you say which missing information matters most?

You are being assessed on the reasoning. A pair that describes this encounter differently from the model, and can say what its answer rests on, has done the exercise better than one that reproduces the tables.
