Confirmation bias

We notice and remember evidence that fits what we already believe, and find reasons to dismiss evidence that does not.

      Anchoring

      A number we have just seen pulls our estimate toward it, even when we know the number is meaningless.

      Question put to both groups

      What percentage of African countries are members of the United Nations?

      Same question, different starting number. Typical answers were 25% after the low anchor and 45% after the high one.

      Before answering, each group watches a wheel stop on a number. Run one group at a time, or both.

      Survivorship bias

      We study what made it through and forget what did not, so the missing data points us to the wrong conclusion.

      Problem put to analysts in World War II

      Bombers come back from missions full of bullet holes. Where should the extra armour go?

      Framing effect

      The same outcome draws a different choice depending on whether it is described as a gain or as a loss.

      Talk it through

      • Which of the excuses in the plant scene sounded most reasonable? When did you last use one yourself?
      • The wheel was plainly random. Why did the number still change people's answers?
      • What habit or process would make you look for disconfirming evidence, or form your own estimate before seeing someone else's number?
      • Where might you be looking only at the planes that came home, such as successful companies, famous artists or people who answer surveys?
      • Think of a recent choice that was described to you as a gain or a loss. How would you have decided if the wording were flipped?

      Anchoring figures come from Tversky and Kahneman (1974), "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases", Science 185. Their wheel was rigged to stop at 10 or 65, and the median answers were 25 and 45. The framing scene follows Tversky and Kahneman (1981), "The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice", Science 211, where about 72% chose the sure option when it was worded as lives saved and about 22% when it was worded as lives lost. The survivorship scene is based on Abraham Wald's analysis of returning bombers for the US military in World War II; the hole pattern drawn here is illustrative. The plant scene is an invented example made for teaching.
